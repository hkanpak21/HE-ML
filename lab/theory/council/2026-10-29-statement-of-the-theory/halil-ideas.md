# Halil's ideas from his meeting with Küpçü

This file paraphrases Halil's own ideas from the meeting. It quotes nothing, and it records none of Küpçü's views as views. Where Halil answered a point raised by Küpçü, the point appears only as far as his answer needs it. The ideas rank below `lab/theory/CONTEXT.md`, Halil's recorded instructions, and what the council and the spaces established. A note after an item says where an established result already bears on it.

1. A learning-constrained attacker should face a sharper bound than a geometric one. Today's guarantees are geometric. A view at a given distance from a record is safe to a given degree, against every function of the view. Halil holds that an attacker who must exploit the signal by learning, and not by an arbitrary function at an arbitrary signal level, meets a tighter bound. He conjectures that the bound is roughly the cost of learning the main task itself. The views he has in mind are gradients in split and federated learning, a signal an attacker plants and later observes (a backdoor), and a watermark. Note: the general form "reconstruction costs what training costs" is refuted (booklet 5.11). For square-loss gradient learners and a Bayes release, extraction costs what training costs (item 16 of the reading of 29 October).

2. The objects. A learning function takes a function family, a dataset, a seed, and hardware randomness. The family is not all functions, nor all parametric functions. It is the special subset of parametric functions that are models, transformers for instance. Hardware randomness is kept so that the object can be realized.

3. What the attacker knows and does. Its side information can include the model family, or less than that, since an attacker may infer the family by comparing how candidate learners behave. It can include partial knowledge of the training data, inferred from the model's behaviour. A generic concept is surely in a large model's training data, since the model is not surprised by it, while a specific local photo may not be. Its observations can be gradients, activations, only the output tokens (a watermarked text), or inference results. Beyond observing, it can act, by steering. It can change training data, or change activations in transit, as a party in the middle of U-shaped split learning can. Halil takes the setting to be this broad, and the attack model to depend on the scenario, with poisoning and model poisoning among them.

4. The objection to existing hardness results. Planted undetectable backdoors, robust learning from computational hardness, and statistically undetectable backdoors in deep networks all reduce to LWE, continuous LWE or sparse PCA. They do so on synthetic Gaussian data with something planted, or by fixing part of the model, such as a frozen random Gaussian compression layer that carries a key. Every setting in which we call a problem hard rests on a notion of random noise, where finding a correlation is hard. Such a target is then also not learnable, because models are gradient optimizers, and a correlation that is hard to find is very hard for them to find. Halil calls the frozen-layer construction an ugly solution, since training a random model never produces a key consistent with the sparse PCA assumption. He allows that a designer who hides such a direction in a released model achieves something real.

5. Real data. Real data lies on a meaningful manifold: photos of ships and houses, human voices. That manifold is not statistically well defined, and it carries no embedded structure of the kind our hard problems need. Our hard problems are indistinguishability problems, and we cannot define meaningful data well enough to state one on it.

6. Homomorphic encryption is set aside on purpose. Encryption moves meaningful data to a well-defined space of ciphertexts, computes there, and moves back. Halil finds encrypting arbitrary computation a remarkable idea, but as an answer to this question it is a meta solution that solves everything at once and says nothing about learning. It is not automatic either, as smudging and the CKKS noise threshold show. The question he keeps is this. A computation leaves a trace in a function space. What can be said about that trace when the data is meaningful data, of the kind we actually use?

7. The random oracle as a measuring device. The oracle evaluates and opens. In a given setting some of its features are withheld. In split learning, for instance, the attacker may receive only the activations of one layer. The oracle can compute an object, such as a dense layer of a large language model trained on a given dataset with a given seed, and hand it over. Halil can then count how many objects the attacker requested, whether they came from one seed or one dataset, and whether it removed or added records, and if it added them, whether it sampled or constructed them. The oracle supplies meaningful objects directly, so one can measure how many samples an attacker needed, and whether it needed more when the model grew. Halil grants that this gives no theoretical bound on the number of runs, and he traces that to the meaningful manifold having no statistical definition.

8. Units of cost. The number of queries, the floating-point operations, and the number of distinct seeds times the number of datasets observed. How these combine into one price is open to him.

9. Hardness must survive learning. If structure is planted in one world and not in the other, and the attacker sees only labels or activations, training in between is post-processing. It only makes the attacker's task harder, and it adds nothing to what is known. A worthwhile result is a task that becomes hard because of learning. Halil also doubts that hardness found in a neighbourhood of the meaningful manifold would survive. A hard problem that gradient optimization nonetheless approximates would be strange, and he takes that possibility as a reason for low confidence.

10. Defences that change honest behaviour. Asking honest clients to plant structure, so that an attacker's planted structure is lost among it, adds poison to the protocol unless a theorem backs it. A constraint that honest parties already meet, such as honest clients adding noise, is realizable and acceptable.

11. A practical constraint, and a capacity price. Pick models in use today, and realize one result across architectures, model sizes, training data and attack scenarios. Then state what the attack needed: a number of queries, or a capacity of the attacker's model relative to the model under attack. Capacity would be read off agreement on held-out data. The attacker's model must match the target's capacity, or exceed it by a stated amount.

12. White box, and what attackers actually do. For an arbitrary attacker holding the weights there is no good bound, not even in restricted split learning. Practitioners do not run arbitrary computations on open weights. They plug the weights into other models through a linear projection or after PCA, and stay close to the model's own basis. An example is the extraction of the last layer of a production language model, followed by stitching with a linear layer. Restricting that class, or measuring it, would suffice. An expectation over seeds or runs, by contrast, is a weak specification, since a plain model can be given an expectation too.

13. Motivation. Halil came to the topic from the question of why transformers work. They compress, and they compute efficiently because they have seen much meaningful data. Learned video compression shows this, where the receiver gets only the points at which the model's prediction departs from the truth. He agrees that the work stays in security and privacy, where many methods exist.

14. The plan Halil agreed to. Return with a problem statement, a constraint close to practical realization, and a theorem to aim at, with the proof to follow. One accepted form of contribution restricts a family of attacks and shows that every member of the family succeeds at most so much on a given family of models. The comparison-sort lower bound has this form.
