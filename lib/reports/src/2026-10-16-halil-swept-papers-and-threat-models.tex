\lrscope
The question is what the sweep of 16 October found at the top venues for
multi-party learning in 2023 to 2026, and how its papers state their
adversaries. We count the search from the screening record, the coded papers by
venue, year, kind, setting, protection and evaluation, and the ledger's
coalitions by seven templates of role, choices and goal. We leave HE-OFT out,
because the study covers the field.

%%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\lrresult
The sweep coded \lrv{Papers} papers, and the typical one studies horizontal
federated learning on MNIST or CIFAR-10 against one of seven adversary
templates (Tables~\ref{tab:papers-a}, \ref{tab:papers-b}
and~\ref{tab:templates}). \lrv{FL} papers train by horizontal federated
learning or federated fine-tuning, \lrv{MnistCifar} evaluate on MNIST or
CIFAR-10, and the templates hold \lrv{Covered} of \lrv{Rows} coalitions. The
privacy line and the robustness line rarely meet in one threat model.
\lrv{UpdNoMech} of \lrv{Upd} rows of clients that steer through their updates
name no confidentiality mechanism, and \lrv{ReadCheck} of \lrv{Read} rows of a
reading server face a check. The coder had to infer static or adaptive
corruption in \lrv{CorrInferred} of \lrv{Rows} rows, and \lrv{CorrAdaptive}
rows have an adaptive adversary (Table~\ref{tab:communities}). Per paper, the
HE share rose from \lrv{HEFirst} to \lrv{HELast} per cent from 2023 to 2026
(Fisher p of \lrv{HEp}), and the deviating share held near \lrv{DevLast} per
cent (Table~\ref{tab:trends}).

\lrboundary{Every count is empirical, on rows that one language-model coder
extracted from each paper, and these rows await a check against their papers.

\lrmethod
\subsection*{The search}
Six language-model screeners read the accepted-paper lists of the eleven venues
for 2023 to 2026, one screener per venue group. A screener kept a paper when two
or more parties train or fine-tune on data or models they hold, and the
contribution concerns privacy or security against an adversary. Each screener
recorded the papers near the rule with a reason, and the screening record holds
\lrv{Candidates} such candidates (Table~\ref{tab:search}). Accepted papers far
from the rule left no record. Screeners dropped \lrv{DroppedScreen} candidates,
most often because one party trains or because the paper states no adversary
(Table~\ref{tab:drops}).

We placed each kept paper with its PDF and a note, after matching its title and
authors on the first page. A coder then read each placed paper and wrote one
ledger row per coalition on 22 columns. It dropped \lrv{DroppedRead} papers as
outside the rule, and \lrv{NoPDF} kept papers wait for an accessible PDF. A row
records what a coalition sees, knows and sets, its goal, the protection, the
checks it faces, the evidence, and any step between two adversaries the paper
settles.

\subsection*{What the coded papers are}
Defences and protocols outnumber attacks, and DP is the most common protection
(Table~\ref{tab:papers-a}). \lrv{NoProtection} papers name no protection for
any coalition, and \lrv{NoProtAttack} of them are attacks and
\lrv{NoProtDefence} defences, \lrv{NoProtRobust} of these by robust aggregation. Clients alone are
the most common coalition, and the three goals appear in similar numbers
(Table~\ref{tab:papers-b}). The evaluations are small. \lrv{Pretrained} papers
start from a public pretrained model, \lrv{LMEval} reach a transformer as their
largest model, and \lrv{NoExperiment} run no experiment.

\subsection*{The seven templates}
A row's template follows from its role, its choices and its goal. The server
alone splits by its choices, and clients alone split by goal and then by
choices. The server with clients forms one template, and a querier or an
eavesdropper outside training forms another. Three rows name a trainer, a
platform operator or no role, and they stay outside.

The server that reads what it receives follows the protocol and inverts or
distinguishes from updates, aggregates and models. DP is its usual protection
and its usual proof (Table~\ref{tab:support}). The papers give this server the
architecture in \lrv{SrvInvertArch} of \lrv{SrvInvert} inverting server rows,
and surrogate data in \lrv{SrvInvertSurrogate}. The two gradient-leakage SoKs
grade this server by levels of knowledge that are not nested.

The server that sets what clients compute sends a crafted model, a changed
architecture, or a different model to each client. An update or a sum of
updates then reveals a client's data. Prose and constructive lower bounds are its most
common forms of evidence. \lrv{S2Stealth} of its \lrv{S2} rows
require or measure stealth, the highest share of any template.

The server with clients is the template of cryptographic protocols, namely
secure aggregation, MPC and multiparty HE. \lrv{SCMech} of its \lrv{SC} rows
name a mechanism, and \lrv{SCCheck} face a check. Its papers prove standalone
simulation security most often. They state static or adaptive corruption in
\lrv{SCStated} rows, the highest share of any template.

Clients that set their updates form the largest template, in the papers' words
Byzantine or malicious clients. The paper assumes an honest majority in
\lrv{C1Honest} of \lrv{Upd} rows, and the usual result is a convergence bound
for a robust aggregation rule. These rows evaluate an adaptive adversary more
often than any other template. Clients that set only their data poison it
before training and follow the protocol after, and their evidence is mostly
empirical.

Clients that read the model learn about another party's data from what they
receive. The template mixes parties of MPC training, which prove simulation
security, nodes of decentralized learning, which prove DP, and parties of
vertical learning. A querier or an eavesdropper observes the trained model or
the messages from outside, most often to distinguish, and DP is again its
usual protection.

\T{support}

\subsection*{What a word means}
The paper's word for its adversary fixes the choices only together with the
role. A malicious server sends a crafted model in \lrv{MalServerModel} of
\lrv{MalServer} rows. Malicious clients set their data in \lrv{MalClientsData}
of \lrv{MalClients} rows and their updates in \lrv{MalClientsOutput}, and
Byzantine clients set their updates in \lrv{ByzOutput} of \lrv{Byz}. The ledger
therefore stores what a coalition sees, knows and sets, and keeps the word as
provenance.

\subsection*{What the papers leave unstated}
Each community states its threat model in its own form
(Table~\ref{tab:communities}). Cryptography papers use an ideal functionality or
a game, learning papers the assumptions of a theorem, and security papers prose
as often as anything else. The coder inferred the corruption model in
\lrv{CorrInferred} rows and the majority in \lrv{MajInferred}. Cryptography
rows are the exception for corruption. Of the \lrv{Steer} steering rows,
\lrv{SteerNoStealth} state no stealth constraint, and \lrv{SteerBenign} say
whether the adversary sees honest updates. \lrv{SteerAdYes} of the
\lrv{SteerAdStated} steering rows that report it evaluate an adaptive adversary.
\lrv{Edges} rows record a step between two adversaries that the paper settles by
a collapse or a separation.

\T{communities}
